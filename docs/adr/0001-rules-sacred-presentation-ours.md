# The classroom game's rules are sacred; presentation is ours

Multiplying Frogs is a port of a board game Connor already plays in his math
class, not a new game inspired by one. So the **rules** of the classroom game —
how far a frog moves, what a wrong answer costs, how many players there are,
what winning means — are preserved exactly, and changing one requires an explicit
decision from Connor. **Presentation** — layout, animation, how something is
tapped, what the screen shows and when — is the project's to design freely, and
needs no such approval.

## Why the line sits there

The reason this game is worth building is that Connor already knows it and likes
it. An eight-year-old will tell you immediately, and with total confidence, when
you have changed his game. A presentation change reads as *"it's the game, on a
phone."* A rule change reads as *"this is a different game now"* — and that is
credibility you get to spend once.

Drawing the line at rules rather than at fidelity-in-general also keeps the
project from photographing a table. Dice that tumble and cards you swipe off a
pile are not what makes the classroom game good; they are what cardboard could
afford. The phone should use its own affordances for those.

## Consequences

- Some interactions will be slightly clunky on a touchscreen because the board
  said so, and smoothing them out is not available as an option.
- "Is this a rule or presentation?" becomes a real question that has to be
  answered per change, and answered in the PR. Two worked examples from the
  design conversation: the app checking answers automatically is **presentation**
  (the classroom game already delegated verification to a calculator), while
  capping v1 at four players is a **rule change** (the rules card says 2–8).
- Rule changes are not forbidden — they need Connor, and they are recorded as
  deviations. Two were made during the founding design conversation and are
  documented as such.
